# Claims & Compliance Reference

This is a working checklist for flagging risk. It is not legal advice. Laws and platform policies change, so when a line could create legal exposure, flag it for the user's counsel and name the specific rule.

## 1. B2B and enterprise claims

**Security and compliance**
- State certifications exactly:
  - "SOC 2 Type II" is not "SOC 2 compliant" and not "SOC 2 certified" (SOC 2 is an attestation report, not a certification).
  - ISO 27001 applies to a defined scope; don't imply it covers the whole company if it doesn't.
  - FedRAMP has impact levels and statuses ("In Process" ≠ "Authorized").
  - HIPAA has no official certification. Say what you actually do (for example, "we sign BAAs").
- Block absolutes: "100% secure", "unhackable", "zero risk", "bank-grade" (unless defined), "fully compliant with all regulations".
- Uptime and performance numbers must point to a contractual SLA or a published status history. Marketing copy must not promise more than the contract does.
- Data claims need specifics: where data is stored, whether customer data trains models, retention periods. Answer these from approved security documentation only. Wrong answers here end up in contracts and security questionnaires.

**Outcomes and ROI**
- No guaranteed results ("will cut costs by 30%").
- Past customer results need context: who the customer was, the baseline, the timeframe, the measure. Phrase them as that customer's result ("Customer X reduced…"), not as a typical result.
- If you imply a result is typical, you need evidence that it is typical. US FTC endorsement guidance treats "results not typical" disclaimers as insufficient.

**Roadmap**
- Never present a future capability as available.
- If the user approves mentioning roadmap, label it "planned" or "on the roadmap", with no date unless an approved date exists, and add a forward-looking caveat when the audience is a customer or buyer. This matters especially for public companies.

**Analyst and award claims**
- Use the exact report name, year and placement ("Named a Leader in the [exact report title], [year]").
- Most analyst firms have citation policies that restrict how placements may be quoted, so check for one.
- Awards carry the year. Expired recognitions get removed.

## 2. Comparative and competitor claims
- A comparison must be truthful, current, verifiable and like-for-like (same tier, same date).
- Date any competitor pricing or feature fact ("as of [month year]") and source it from a public page you actually opened.
- Never disparage, speculate about a competitor's finances, security or legal troubles, or repeat unverified rumors. Defamation risk lands on the sender.
- **EU:** comparative advertising must compare material, relevant, verifiable and representative features, and must not denigrate (Directive 2006/114/EC).
- **UK:** the CAP Code is similar.
- Superlatives that imply comparison ("fastest", "#1", "most accurate") need third-party or rigorous internal substantiation that covers the market as a whole.

## 3. Customer names, logos, testimonials and reviews
- Use a customer name, logo, quote or case study only with recorded permission. The proof library records the permission level: `public`, `named in 1:1 only`, `anonymized only` or `internal only`.
- Many enterprise contracts forbid publicity without written consent. Public-company customers may also need their own legal or investor-relations review.
- Anonymize properly. "A Fortune 50 retailer headquartered in Arkansas" is not anonymous. Round the figures or use ratios when the customer asks.
- Testimonials must reflect the person's honest, actual words and experience. Light editing for length is fine; adding claims or numbers the customer didn't make is not.
- Disclose material connections: paid, incentivized, employee, partner or investor relationships.
- **US FTC rule on fake reviews and testimonials (16 CFR Part 465, effective October 2024):**
  - Bans fake or AI-fabricated reviews and testimonials.
  - Bans buying positive or negative reviews.
  - Bans undisclosed insider reviews.
  - Bans review suppression.
  - Bans fake social-proof indicators.
- **EU:** submitting or commissioning fake reviews is banned, and so is claiming that reviews come from real customers without taking reasonable steps to verify it (Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, Annex I points 23b–23c).
- **UK:** fake reviews and concealed incentivized reviews are banned under the DMCCA 2024 (since April 2025).
- **No review gating.** Don't ask only happy customers for public reviews. Moderation criteria (PII, profanity, off-topic) are applied the same way at every rating; suppressing negative reviews is prohibited (16 CFR 465.7).
- **Platform incentive rules differ.** Google prohibits incentives. G2 and Capterra run or approve disclosed, sentiment-neutral incentive programs under their own rules. Amazon prohibits incentivized reviews outside its own programs (such as Vine). Check each platform's current policy before offering anything.

## 4. Pricing and discount claims
- Prices must match the current price list or the quote. Quotes in proposals carry a validity date.
- Never imply a discount expires when it doesn't (fake urgency). Never show a "was" price that wasn't genuinely charged.
- **EU** (Price Indication Directive, Article 6a, added by the Omnibus Directive): an announced price reduction must reference the lowest price applied in the previous 30 days or more.
- **US:** the FTC Guides Against Deceptive Pricing apply, and some states have stricter reference-price rules.
- Don't put internal discount floors, approval thresholds or other customers' pricing into external copy.

## 5. Product, environmental and regulated-category claims (PDP-heavy)
- **Substantiation.** In the US, objective product claims need a "reasonable basis" before they are made. Health and safety claims generally need competent and reliable scientific evidence.
- **Environmental claims.**
  - US: FTC Green Guides. Avoid unqualified "eco-friendly", "green" or "sustainable". "Recyclable" depends on access to recycling facilities. "Carbon neutral" needs disclosed methodology.
  - US: carbon offset or "carbon neutral" claims need competent and reliable scientific and accounting methods. They must disclose if the offset reductions won't happen for two or more years, and can't rely on reductions the law already requires.
  - EU (consumer-facing claims): the Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive ((EU) 2024/825) has applied since 27 September 2026, through each member state's national law. It bans:
    - generic environmental claims ("eco-friendly", "green", "climate friendly") unless recognized excellent environmental performance can be shown
    - claims that a product has a neutral, reduced or positive environmental impact based on offsetting
    - sustainability labels not based on a certification scheme or set by public authorities

    Treat "climate neutral thanks to offsets" as blocked for EU consumers.
- **"Free-from" and chemical claims** (BPA-free, PFAS-free, lead-free, phthalate-free, non-toxic, hypoallergenic, chemical-free):
  - Allowed only when backed by test data or supplier certification for that specific SKU, and only in the approved wording.
  - "Non-toxic" and "chemical-free" are almost always misleading. Avoid them.
  - Free-from claims on children's or food-contact products are safety-adjacent, so treat them as high sensitivity.
- **Origin.** "Made in USA" requires "all or virtually all" US content (FTC Made in USA Labeling Rule). Qualified claims must be specific.
- **Supplements, food and cosmetics.**
  - No disease claims ("cures", "treats", "prevents") for supplements or cosmetics.
  - Structure/function claims on US supplements need the required FDA disclaimer.
  - Don't say "FDA approved" for products the FDA doesn't approve. Cosmetics and supplements are not FDA-approved.
  - "Clinically proven" requires actual clinical evidence for that claim.
  - Allergen and nutrition statements must match the label exactly.
- **Children's products.** Safety and certification claims must match test reports (for example CPSIA in the US, CE/UKCA where relevant).
- **Warranties and guarantees.** State the terms accurately and link to them. Don't call something "lifetime" unless the terms say so.
- **Certifications and marks** (UL, Energy Star, organic, Fair Trade, B Corp): only if current, and only for the specific SKU.
- **Consistency across channels.** The same claim must read the same on the PDP, marketplace listing, ad and packaging. A slow drift ("recycled plastic" → "100% recycled" → "fully sustainable") is a common way products end up making claims nobody substantiated.

**CPG and product claims in depth** (classification, FDA, EPA, EU, Green Guides, reviews and adverse events, retailer antitrust): `industry-rules/cpg-product-claims.md`.

## 6. Regulated industries
Flag for specialist review when the **seller** or the **audience** is in one of these:
- **Financial services:**
  - No performance guarantees.
  - Past-performance disclaimers.
  - The SEC Marketing Rule for investment advisers.
  - FINRA communications rules for broker-dealers.
  - APR and fee disclosure next to rates.
- **Healthcare and life sciences:** HIPAA (never confirm someone is a patient; no PHI in marketing); FDA promotional rules (on-label only, fair balance).
- **Insurance:** state-specific advertising rules.
- **Legal services:** no outcome guarantees; bar advertising rules.
- **Public sector:**
  - Procurement integrity and gift rules.
  - No contact during blackout or quiet periods of active solicitations, except through the designated contact.
  - Lobbying disclosures where they apply.
- **Pharma, alcohol, cannabis, gambling, firearms:** heavy platform and jurisdiction restrictions. Defer to the user's compliance team.

A regulated-industry asset is always **External – high sensitivity**. Detailed rules:
- `industry-rules/financial-services-marketing.md`: FINRA 2210, SEC Marketing Rule, Reg BI, UDAAP, Reg Z/DD, fair lending, UK/EU.
- `industry-rules/healthcare-marketing.md`: HIPAA marketing, testimonials, tracking pixels, FDA/FTC health claims, inducements, payer marketing, accessibility, Section 1557, AI patient communications.

## 7. Outreach and privacy law (verify current rules per market)
- **Commercial vs. relationship email.** These requirements apply to commercial messages: cold outreach, sequences, marketing and bulk sends. A 1:1 reply in an ongoing conversation with an engaged buyer generally follows the company's normal signature policy, not a marketing footer. When unsure, ask the user or their compliance team.
- **US, CAN-SPAM:**
  - Accurate From and subject lines.
  - Identify the message as an advertisement or solicitation unless the recipient gave prior affirmative consent.
  - A physical postal address.
  - A working opt-out that stays functional for 30 days after sending, honored within 10 business days.
  - Applies to B2B email.
- **US, TCPA:**
  - Telemarketing calls or texts sent with an autodialer, or using an artificial or prerecorded voice, need prior express written consent. AI-generated voices count as artificial (FCC, February 2024).
  - Honor the National Do Not Call Registry and internal do-not-call lists.
  - Federal calling hours are 8 a.m.–9 p.m. in the recipient's local time. Some states are stricter.
  - Treat all marketing SMS as needing written consent.
- **Canada, CASL:**
  - Consent is required for commercial electronic messages.
  - Implied consent lasts 2 years from a purchase, or 6 months from an inquiry.
  - A conspicuously published electronic address, with no "no unsolicited messages" statement, implies consent only for messages relevant to the recipient's business role.
  - Identification and a working unsubscribe are required; process unsubscribes within 10 business days.
- **EU/EEA, GDPR + ePrivacy.** Two layers apply.
  1. **GDPR** sets the lawful basis for processing the data. This is often legitimate interest, with a documented balancing test, data minimization, a stated source and an easy opt-out.
  2. **National ePrivacy law** decides whether marketing email needs prior consent, and it overrides legitimate interest on that question.
     - Several member states require prior consent even for B2B email, for example Germany, Austria, Italy, Spain, Poland and Denmark.
     - Others allow B2B email to work addresses with an opt-out.
     - Check the recipient's country before any cold email, and confirm with counsel.
- **UK, UK GDPR + PECR:**
  - Corporate subscribers can be emailed with an opt-out.
  - Sole traders and some partnerships are treated as individuals and need consent.
- **LinkedIn:** don't use third-party tools to automate connection requests, messages or scraping, because they breach LinkedIn's User Agreement. The agent drafts; a human sends.
- **Call recording and transcripts:** use them only if the call was recorded with the consent each participant's jurisdiction requires. Some US states and many countries require all-party consent.
- **Personal data hygiene:**
  - Professional, publicly available business information only.
  - No personal phone numbers, home addresses, family details or health information.
  - No scraping behind logins, or in breach of a site's terms.
  - Honor opt-outs across every sequence and channel.
- **Ad audiences:** uploading contact lists requires consent or a lawful basis that covers advertising use, not just email. Don't upload purchased or broker data without verifying its consent basis.

## 8. AI-generated content disclosure
- **EU AI Act Article 50** (transparency obligations apply from 2 August 2026; verify the current status, since EU simplification measures have adjusted some related deadlines):
  - **Art. 50(1):** an AI system that interacts directly with people, such as a chat assistant or an autonomous reply agent, must make clear that they are dealing with an AI, unless it's obvious.
  - **Text on public-interest matters:** deployers who publish AI-generated or AI-manipulated text to inform the public on matters of public interest must disclose it. This doesn't apply if the text went through human review or editorial control and a person holds editorial responsibility.
  - **Deepfakes:** deepfake images, audio and video must be disclosed.
  - **Out of scope, generally:** ordinary product or marketing copy that humans review. Confirm with counsel for public-interest or news-like content.
- **US bot disclosure:** California's B.O.T. Act (Bus. & Prof. Code §17940 et seq.) requires disclosing a bot that is used to incentivize a sale online. An AI agent conversing with buyers in real time must never claim to be human.
- Never present AI-generated reviews, testimonials, quotes or personas as real people.
- When ghostwriting for an executive, the executive must review and approve the final words. Don't publish under their name without that approval.

## 9. Order of precedence when rules conflict
1. Law in the recipient's or buyer's jurisdiction. The strictest applicable rule wins. If the jurisdiction is unknown, assume GDPR-level privacy and FTC-level advertising standards.
2. Contractual obligations: customer publicity clauses, NDAs, partner agreements.
3. Industry regulation.
4. Platform and retailer policies.
5. The user's brand restrictions.
6. Style and voice preferences.
