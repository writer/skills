# Twelve Best Practices for Executive Presentations

These are the audit criteria. Read them before auditing a deck. For each, check the user's presentation against the checklist and report what is working, what is broken, and how to fix it with specific rewrites.

---

## 1. Lead with the recommendation, not the journey (Pyramid Principle)

The Pyramid Principle, developed by Barbara Minto at McKinsey, inverts how most people present. You think bottom-up: gather data, run analysis, reach a conclusion. But you present top-down: answer first, then the arguments that support it, then the evidence underneath each argument. The single governing thought of the deck, the recommendation, belongs on slide one or two, not buried at the end.

**Check for:**
- Does the executive summary state the recommendation and the core argument in the first 30 seconds of reading?
- Can a time-pressed executive read only the first one or two slides and understand what is being asked for and why?
- Or is the punchline buried behind context, methodology, or background?

If the recommendation is not stated up front, rewrite the opening so it is.

---

## 2. Set up the story with SCQA

McKinsey, Bain, and BCG use the SCQA framework to give context before the answer:

- **Situation**: where things stand today (the audience already knows this; confirm shared understanding)
- **Complication**: what changed, what is broken, what created the tension that demands a decision
- **Question**: the decision the presentation answers (e.g., "Should we enter the European market?")
- **Answer**: the recommendation and its supporting logic

The situation and complication should be brief: a few sentences, not slides of background. The question makes the rest of the deck feel inevitable. The answer is the recommendation.

**Check for:**
- Do the situation and complication make the question feel urgent?
- Is the question explicit? Does the audience know what decision this presentation answers?
- Or did the presenter jump into analysis without setting up why it matters?

---

## 3. Use action titles: every slide title is a complete sentence with a point

In top consulting decks, slide titles are not labels. They are complete sentences that state the slide's conclusion. "Market Overview" is a label. "The German market is growing 12% annually, 3x the rate of North America" is an action title.

The test: if an executive reads only the titles of every slide in order, they should understand the full argument without seeing a single chart or bullet. This is the "titles test," and it is how partners at consulting firms review decks.

**Check for:**
- Does every slide title state a conclusion, not a topic?
- Read the titles in sequence: do they tell a coherent story? Or are there gaps, jumps, or slides where the title could mean anything?
- Rewrite every title that is a label into an action title.

---

## 4. One message per slide: no exceptions

Each slide makes exactly one point. That point lives in the action title. Everything else on the slide (charts, bullets, callouts) exists to support that one point and answer "Why should I believe the title?"

If a title contains "and," there are probably two slides. If a slide tries to prove two things, the title can only state one, and the slide stops being a defensible unit of argument.

**Check for:**
- Does any slide try to make two or more points? Split it.
- Is everything on each slide in service of the title? Cut anything that is not.
- Is any slide a "data dump": raw numbers or a full table with no stated implication? Give it a message or move it to the appendix.

---

## 5. Data serves the message: it is not the message

Bain decks lead with the message and use data to back it. The slide makes a statement, then shows the specific data that supports it. Never dump a table onto a slide and hope the audience draws the conclusion.

Weak: "Revenue numbers Q1 to Q4" followed by a spreadsheet.
Strong: "Revenue is declining quarter over quarter, driven by a sharp drop in repeat customers" followed by one clean chart highlighting the trend.

**Check for:**
- Does every chart or table have a stated implication in the title or a callout?
- Is any data presented without context: a single number with no comparison, trend, or benchmark? A number without context is noise.
- Are the charts clean: one key takeaway highlighted, not five lines in five colors all fighting for attention?

---

## 6. Write in active voice: be concise, be direct

Active voice puts the actor before the action: "Marketing will launch the campaign in Q2" not "The campaign will be launched by Marketing in Q2." Active voice is shorter, clearer, and assigns ownership. Executives want to know who is doing what.

Cut hedging language. Remove qualifiers that soften without adding meaning: "somewhat," "relatively," "fairly," "to some extent." If the data supports a claim, make it. If it does not, do not make it and then hedge.

**Check for:**
- Flag every passive construction and rewrite it in active voice.
- Flag every hedge and either remove it or replace it with a specific qualifier backed by data.
- Are the sentences short? If a sentence runs past two lines, break it.

---

## 7. Make trade-offs explicit: no recommendation is free

Every recommendation has costs. Top consultants name them. If the presenter recommends Option A, they should show what Option A costs: what they give up, what risk they accept, what alternative they reject. Hiding trade-offs does not strengthen a recommendation; it makes the audience distrust it.

Bain's logical flow follows this rhythm: define the problem, show the evidence, break down options, compare trade-offs, recommend a path forward. The audience should feel they arrived at the recommendation themselves, not that it was handed to them.

**Check for:**
- Did the presenter show alternatives and explain why they rejected them?
- Did they name the risks and costs of the recommendation, or present it as all upside?
- Is the comparison between options structured and clear (criteria-based, not narrative)?

---

## 8. Build a logical flow: each slide earns the next

The deck should feel like a guided argument, not a collection of slides. Each slide answers a question that the previous slide raised. By the time the presenter reaches the recommendation, the audience should feel it was inevitable: they arrived there together.

Structure the deck so that the argument builds: context, evidence, options, trade-offs, recommendation, next steps. If a slide does not advance the argument, it belongs in the appendix.

**Check for:**
- Read the deck start to finish: does each slide follow logically from the one before? Are there jumps where the audience has to guess how the presenter got from A to B?
- Is there a slide that is interesting but not necessary? Move it to the appendix.
- Does the deck end with clear next steps: who does what by when?

---

## 9. Apply visual discipline: restraint over decoration

Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30 rule: ten slides, twenty minutes, thirty-point font. The point is not the exact numbers; it is the discipline. If someone needs forty slides to make their case, they probably do not have a case. Small font means cramming, and cramming means they do not know what matters.

Garr Reynolds (Presentation Zen): have a clear theme. If the audience remembers one thing, what is it? Remove the nonessential. If it does not serve the argument, cut it.

Color with intention: use color to direct attention, not for decoration. Charts on the left, text on the right (people read left to right; the visual gives the intuitive impression, the text gives the logic).

**Check for:**
- How many slides are there? Could the argument survive with fewer? Where can the presenter cut?
- Is the font large enough to read from the back of the room? Is the presenter cramming text?
- Is there a single key takeaway the audience should remember? Is it stated clearly and repeatedly?
- Is color used deliberately or randomly?

---

## 10. Pass the skim test: assume they will not read everything

Executives skim. They read the title, glance at the chart, and move on. Build for that. The action title carries the message. The chart confirms it. The bullets are backup. If someone spends ten seconds per slide, do they get the argument?

Nancy Duarte's insight: the presenter is not the hero. The audience is the hero. The presenter is the mentor: Yoda, not Luke. The job is to give the audience what they need to make the decision, not to show them how hard the presenter worked.

**Check for:**
- Skim the deck: titles only, ten seconds per slide. Is the argument clear?
- Is the key chart on each slide immediately readable, or does it require study?
- Is the recommendation findable without reading every word?

---

## 11. Make the stakes clear: why this matters now

Carmine Gallo: a big idea has two parts: what action to take and what is at stake. If the audience does not feel the cost of inaction, they will not act. Frame the stakes: what happens if we do nothing? What is the cost of waiting? What opportunity are we leaving on the table?

Duarte's structure moves between "what is" and "what could be." Show the gap between the current state and the desired state. Make the audience feel the distance. Then show them the bridge.

**Check for:**
- Is the cost of inaction explicit?
- Is the stakes framing concrete (dollars, market share, time, competitive risk) or vague ("we risk falling behind")?
- Does the audience feel urgency, or does this feel like a nice-to-have?

---

## 12. End with action: not summary

The last slide is the ask. What does the presenter want the audience to do, decide, or approve, specifically, with a timeline and an owner? If the deck ends with "Questions?" or "Thank you," the most important slide has been wasted.

**Check for:**
- Does the final slide state the decision being asked for?
- Are next steps specific: who, what, by when?
- Or does the deck trail off into a summary of what was already said?
