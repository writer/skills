# Competitive & Objections

Enterprise buyers check what you say. Reps who stay credible about competitors, including admitting where a competitor is stronger, win more trust than reps who trash them. Objections are usually information about what the buyer needs, not attacks to defeat.

## Part A — Competitive intelligence

### Collection rules
- **Only report what you actually opened.** Every fact carries a URL, the date observed, a confirmed/inferred label and confidence. For pricing and messaging, quote verbatim.
- **Public sources only.** Use competitor sites, docs, pricing pages, release notes, review sites (patterns, not single posts), job posts, filings and analyst summaries. Never use material behind a login, leaked documents, or anything obtained by misrepresentation.
- **"Unknown" beats a guess.** Never estimate a competitor's revenue, customers, pricing or roadmap without a cited source.
- **Single-source claims are flagged.** Rumors stay out of anything customer-facing.
- **Always include the non-vendor alternatives:** do nothing, build in-house, agencies or services, and reallocating budget to a different priority. In enterprise deals, "no decision" is often the biggest competitor.

### Battle card (internal; 1–2 pages; dated; refresh quarterly or on a major competitor release)
```
# <Competitor> — Battle Card · as of <date> · CONFIDENTIAL — internal only
## In one line: how they position themselves (verbatim) vs. how buyers actually see them (evidence)
## When we win / when we lose (from real win/loss data; mark if hypothesis)
## Their genuine strengths (3–5) — and how we respond to each without denying it
## Their weaknesses (verified, specific, sourced — not rumors)
## Our differentiators that matter to buyers (each with approved proof ID)
## Landmine questions (genuinely useful discovery questions that surface where we're stronger)
## Likely objections when they're in the deal + responses
## Traps they set for us + how to handle
## Proof: relevant approved customer stories, switch stories
## Don't say: claims we can't substantiate; anything disparaging
## Sources
```

**Landmine questions** must be questions a good advisor would ask anyway. They should surface criteria where we really are stronger. Example: "How will you keep product content consistent across retailers when attributes change?" They must not be thinly disguised attacks. If a question only makes sense as a trap, drop it.

**Positioning line:** "Where [competitor] is strong at [real strength], we focus on [differentiator], which matters if [buyer situation]."

### Responding to competitor moves

| Move | Response | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| Price cut | Re-anchor on value and total cost (implementation, services, internal effort, risk). Equip reps with TCO talk tracks. Hold price in segments where we win on value | Match by reflex; start a price war |
| Feature launch | Assess what it actually does (docs, not the press release). Decide: parity, differentiate, or ignore. Brief sales with an honest answer | Claim "we already do that" unless it's verifiably true |
| Negative comparison content about us | Respond with factual, balanced comparison content; correct factual errors directly and calmly | Attack back; make unverifiable counterclaims |
| Funding or acquisition | Reassure our customers on our stability and focus; watch for roadmap and price changes at the competitor | Spread fear about their future |
| Competitor crisis (outage, breach) | Stay quiet publicly; support any affected prospects who ask | Exploit it in marketing |

### Comparison and alternative pages (public)
- **Structure:**
  1. A summary for who each option is best for.
  2. A dated comparison table.
  3. Where we're stronger.
  4. **Where they're stronger, stated honestly.**
  5. Switching considerations and migration help.
  6. FAQ.
  7. A call to action.
- Every competitor claim is public, sourced, dated and fair. Tables include rows where we lose.
- Legal review before publishing. Comparative-advertising rules apply; see the claims reference in `customer-facing-quality-gate`.

### Win/loss analysis
- Interview soon after the decision, through a neutral interviewer where possible. Talk to winners and losers, and to "no decision" outcomes.
- **Ask:**
  - Why change at all?
  - Who was involved?
  - What criteria mattered, and how were they weighted?
  - When and why did each vendor get eliminated?
  - What almost changed the outcome?
- **Code the reasons:** value or ROI, capability gap, price or commercial terms, trust or risk, relationship and sales process, timing or priority, integration, incumbent advantage.
- Prioritize fixes by frequency × deal value × how addressable the reason is.
- Patterns need multiple deals. One loss is an anecdote. Say how many deals support each conclusion.

## Part B — Objection handling

### Method: diagnose before responding
1. **Listen fully.** Don't interrupt, and don't defend.
2. **Acknowledge** it in plain words, without "but" ("Makes sense. You're comparing it to the agency line, not zero."). Ask hard questions directly ("Was it the number?").
3. **Explore** with a diagnostic question. The stated objection is often not the real one.
4. **Respond** to the real concern with evidence (approved proof only) or an offer that reduces risk.
5. **Confirm:** "Does that address it, or is there more behind it?"

### Common enterprise objections

**"It's too expensive."**
- Diagnose: "Is it higher than you expected, or is budget for this category not secured yet?"
- Then decide which conversation you're in:
  - **Value:** rebuild the ROI in their numbers, compare against cost of inaction and total cost of alternatives.
  - **Budget:** phase it, reduce scope, or time it to their budget cycle.
  - **Negotiation tactic:** hold and trade (see the give/get rules in `business-case-and-proposal`, §5).

**"No budget this year."**
- Is it truly none, or not allocated? Ask: "If the business case were clear, where would funding come from?" Could existing spend be reallocated (agencies, manual effort, an incumbent tool)?
- Plan for their budget cycle.
- Walk away for now if the economic buyer confirms there is no priority.

**"Not the right time" / "not a priority."**
- "What *is* the priority?" Look for the connection between our value and their top priority.
- If there's no link, agree a revisit date and stay useful in the meantime.

**"We'll build it in-house."**
- Respect the option. Sometimes it's right, especially when the capability is their core differentiator.
- Explore the full cost honestly: build, maintenance, opportunity cost of the engineers, time to value, risk.
- Offer a fair comparison. **Don't quote invented build-vs-buy multipliers.**

**"We're happy with our current vendor" / "we're under contract."**
- Ask: "What would make it even better?" and "When does the contract renew, and when does evaluation start?"
- Plant criteria now. Re-engage well before the renewal window.
- Never imply they made a bad decision.

**"Your product is missing X."**
- Ask how often they'd use it and what it enables. Separate must-have from nice-to-have.
- If it's a hard requirement we can't meet, say so: "If X is a must-have, we're not the right fit today."
- Credibility matters more than this one deal.

**Security, privacy and compliance concerns.**
- Treat them as legitimate.
- Bring the approved security package: certifications stated exactly, documentation, the DPA.
- Offer to run the security review in parallel with evaluation.
- Never improvise answers. Use "I'll confirm in writing with our security team" and follow up.

**AI-specific risk** (accuracy, hallucination, data use, IP, brand safety, regulation).
- Ask what specifically worries them and who raised it: legal, security, brand, or the executive team.
- Answer with how the product actually controls the risk: grounding in their data, review workflows, guardrails, data-use terms, audit trails.
- Use approved documentation and proof only.
- Propose a pilot with explicit success and risk criteria that the concerned stakeholder helps define.

**"Our agreements don't let us share retailer data"** (POS, loyalty, clean-room outputs):
- Treat it as legitimate. Ask which data, under which agreement, and who owns derived insights.
- Offer what fits within their rights: brand-owned data, the retailer's own clean room, or approved-vendor programs.
- Never suggest a workaround.

**"You'll never get through our vendor risk / model risk / BAA review"** (banks, insurers, health systems):
- Agree that the review is real. Ask for their process, questionnaire and timeline.
- Offer to start it in parallel with evaluation, and supply the approved security package.
- Never claim regulators "approve" vendors. Say "we provide documentation to support your review".
- See the due-diligence sections in `account-targeting-and-research/references/industry-playbooks/`.

**"Send me some information."**
- Often a polite brush-off, sometimes a genuine request. Ask what they'd look for and who else will read it.
- Send one tailored item and propose a specific follow-up.

**"I need to talk to my boss / the team."**
- Offer help: a short internal summary, a business-case one-pager, or a joint meeting.
- Ask what the boss or team will care about most.
- If it has been weeks with no access to the decision-maker, the deal is stalled. Treat it in `enterprise-deal-strategy`.

**Procurement pressure** ("we need 30% off to sign this quarter").
- Clarify what is being asked and why: benchmark, budget gap, or standard procurement practice.
- Trade, don't give. See negotiation preparation in `business-case-and-proposal`.

### Guardrails
- **Never fabricate proof points to answer an objection.** Objection scripts often include "[Customer] saw X%" placeholders. Fill them only from the proof library, or remove them.
- **Never disparage a competitor or a prior vendor.**
- **When the honest answer is "we're not the right fit", say it.**
