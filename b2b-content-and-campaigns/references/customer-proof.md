# Customer Proof: Case Studies & Testimonials

## Intake
Ask for anything that's missing. If the user wants a draft now, write it with visible `[NEEDS DETAIL]` / `[NEEDS APPROVAL]` markers, list the questions, and never fill a gap with an invented detail.

1. **Customer.** Company, industry and size. Champion's name and title.
2. **Before.** The situation, the problem, and what it cost them.
3. **Trigger.** Why they acted then.
4. **What they did.** The approach, the people involved, how long it took. Describe moves, not features.
5. **After.** At least one measured result, with baseline, timeframe and how it was measured.
6. **Quote.** The customer's real words.
7. **Permission level.** `public`, `named in 1:1 only`, `anonymized only` or `internal only` (same levels as the proof library). Also: who approves, and whether their legal, PR or investor-relations team must review.
8. **Use cases.** Where the proof will be used: web, sales deck, RFPs, events.

If the outcome is vague ("things got better"), ask for one number. A rough number is fine if the customer stands behind it ("about 5 hours a week"). If there's still no number, use honest qualitative language. **Never estimate a number for them.**

## Results language by evidence strength
| Evidence | Allowed language |
|---|---|
| Measured metric, customer-confirmed | State it directly with baseline and period: "cut time to publish from 15 days to 3 in two quarters" |
| Qualitative change, customer-confirmed | "For the first time, the team could…" |
| Contribution among several factors | "Contributed to…", "Helped…" |
| Directional / anecdotal | Don't paraphrase it into a claim. Use it only inside the customer's approved quote, or turn it into a follow-up to measure. |

Show both the relative and the absolute figure where possible ("62% faster: 15 → 3 days"). Percentages alone hide the scale.

## Making it impressive, honestly
When asked to "make it impressive", lead with the strongest **real** result, made concrete ("month-end close in 4 days instead of 9").

**Settle uncertainty before publishing, not in the copy.** Have the customer confirm the number once, then state it cleanly.

**Ask the questions that produce copy:**
- What did a bad week look like before?
- What would doing nothing have cost?
- What did the time saved let you do?
- What do you tell peers about it?

**Emotion plus reason.** Pair the champion's personal win ("we stopped working weekends") with the business number.

**Quotes.** If a strong short line sits inside a cliché ("a total game changer, we stopped working weekends"), ask approval to quote just the short line.

**Scope.** Never put the catalog size next to a result that covered only part of it.

Unmeasured outcomes (e.g., returns "felt lower") become a follow-up to measure, not a claim.

Retailers, marketplaces and partners mentioned in the story must not be presented as endorsing us unless they have approved it.

## Story principles
- **The customer is the hero; we are the guide.** Tell it from their perspective, around their goals and decisions.
- **Tension is essential.** With no real challenge there is no story. Include what was hard, including what didn't go perfectly, if the customer is comfortable with that.
- **Specific beats glowing.** Names, numbers and concrete details carry the credibility. Superlatives don't.

## Formats (build all three from one interview)
1. **Two-liner**, for decks, RFPs and emails: "[Customer descriptor] used [us] to [outcome]. [Measured metric + timeframe, or the qualitative change if nothing was measured]."
2. **Story**, 150–250 words, for sales conversations and LinkedIn: situation → complication → what changed → result → quote.
3. **Full case study**, for web, PDF and RFP appendices.
   - **Headline:** the result as a plain before/after ("nine days to four"), or the customer's best line. Write 5+ options and pick the one a peer would forward.
   - **First screen:** the stakes in the customer's world, the result, and the best quote.
   - **Then:** what was hard → what they did → results (relative and absolute) → **one next step for a reader with the same problem**, using an offer the company actually provides.

## Testimonials
- **Ask in a way that makes it easy:**
  - Offer three questions: what was the situation before, what changed, and would you recommend it and why.
  - Or offer to draft it from their own words for their approval.
- **When drafting from their words:**
  - Use only claims and numbers they actually gave.
  - Match how they actually speak.
  - No superlatives they didn't use.
  - They approve the final text in writing.
- **Quality check (1–5 each):**
  - specificity
  - measurability
  - authentic voice
  - usable length across formats

  Publish when specificity, authentic voice and length all score 4 or higher. Measurability is required only for metric-led proof.
- **Ask everyone the same way.** Don't ask only the happiest customers for public reviews (review gating). Platform rules and the FTC rule apply. See the claims reference in `customer-facing-quality-gate`.

## Approval workflow
1. Draft.
2. Internal review (accuracy, voice).
3. **Customer review.** Give them time and the right to edit or redact.
4. Customer's written approval, plus their legal or PR sign-off where required. Public-company customers often need this.
5. Record the proof in the proof library with its permission level and a review date.
6. Re-confirm before reusing it after 12–18 months, or if the champion leaves.
