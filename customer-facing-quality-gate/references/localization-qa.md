# Localization QA (multi-market assets)

Use this for any asset adapted for another language or market: emails, web pages, PDPs, campaigns, decks.

## 1. Classify each content element before translating it
| Element type | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Factual** (specs, pricing, terms, legal, product attributes) | Translate precisely. Convert units, currency, date and number formats. Never "adapt" a fact. |
| **Persuasive** (headlines, CTAs, taglines, hooks) | Transcreate. Offer 2–3 options, each with a back-translation into the source language and a note on intent and cultural fit. |
| **Compliance-sensitive** (disclaimers, claims, consent text, warranty) | Use the market's legally required wording, which comes from local counsel or the client. Don't freestyle it. |
| **SEO-dependent** (titles, meta, PDP keywords) | Research local search language. Direct keyword translation is often wrong. |

## 2. Hard checks (any failure blocks)
- **Do-not-translate terms are preserved exactly:** brand, product and feature names, trademarks.
- **Placeholders and variables are intact:** `{first_name}`, URLs, merge tags, HTML.
- **Numbers, prices, dates and units are correct for the market.** Watch decimal separators: 1.000,50 vs 1,000.50.
- **No claim exists in the localized version that doesn't exist in the source.** Translators and models both add flourish.
- **Market-required elements are present and themselves localized, not left in English.** Examples: Germany's Impressum, consent language, required price displays (such as India's MRP), and right-to-left layout for Arabic, Hebrew, Farsi and Urdu.
- **Length fits the layout.** German and French typically expand 10–35% over English. Chinese, Japanese and Korean typically contract. Check character limits on titles, buttons and marketplace fields.

## 3. Review decision
- Any hard-check failure: fix it and re-check.
- Meaning, fluency and brand voice are judged qualitatively, and the issues are listed specifically (e.g., "'BrandName Pro' rendered as 'BrandName Profi' in line 3").
- Customer-facing assets in a market where the agent cannot verify fluency always get a native-speaker review before publishing, as do new markets and new categories. Don't invent a fluency score as a substitute.

## 3b. Market pointers (flag for local counsel; don't apply from memory)

| Market | Rules to flag |
|---|---|
| EU | Food information (1169/2011), cosmetics (1223/2009), GPSR listing information |
| UK | Diverging from EU rules over time |
| Canada | English and French labeling |
| Germany | HWG (health advertising), UWG |
| Mexico | NOM-051 front-of-pack warnings |
| Australia | TGA, FSANZ |

- **Back-translate every critical claim,** not only the persuasive lines.
- **Bilingual markets** (Canada, Belgium, Switzerland): every language gets equal creative quality.
- **Spelling variants** (e.g., color/colour) belong in search terms.

## 4. Cultural adaptation notes (heuristics, not rules)
- **Proof preferences differ.** Some markets weight certifications, specifications and technical data most heavily (for example DACH and Japan). Others weight peer references and relationships. Choose proof accordingly.
- **Directness and humor travel poorly.** Remove idioms and wordplay rather than translating them.
- **Urgency and superlative norms differ.** Some markets restrict superlatives more tightly than the US does.
- **Calendars and working weeks differ.** Check local holidays and working days before scheduling sends. Some Middle Eastern markets use a Friday–Saturday or Saturday–Sunday weekend depending on the country.
