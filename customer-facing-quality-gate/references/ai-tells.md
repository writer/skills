# AI-Tell Editing Guide

Prose that sounds machine-written makes enterprise buyers trust the sender less. The worst offenders are cold emails and executive bylines, where the reader is deciding whether a real person thought about them.

**How to use this list:** treat it as a guide for where to focus editing, not as a blocking gate. Surface word lists are weak detectors. Plenty of human writers use "robust" and plenty of AI text avoids it. What reliably separates good writing from AI filler is **specificity**: real names, numbers, quotes and concrete situations. When a sentence is flagged, fix it by adding a verified specific or by cutting it. Swapping in a synonym doesn't fix it, and inventing a detail is far worse than leaving the sentence vague.

## 0. Write it right first (the positive craft)
Banning words doesn't fix generic writing. Copy sounds machine-made when it has no position and no specifics. Before writing, get three things:
- **The reader, exactly.** The person, their problem, and the moment it bites.
- **The one claim** the piece makes, stated in a single sentence.
- **Three specifics only this piece could contain.** A real number, a named thing, or the buyer's own words.

**Then draft fast and cut hard:**
1. Delete the warm-up opening and start where the specifics start.
2. Make every generic sentence specific, or kill it.
3. Read it aloud and vary the rhythm. A short sentence after two long ones lands.
4. Cut about 30%: hedges, adverbs, a second example once the first has landed, and any sentence that restates the one before it.
5. End on the sharpest specific or a real question. Never end on a summary.

**Don't swap corporate filler for trendy filler.** Wall-to-wall fragments, forced contrarian turns and "here's the thing" are the same emptiness in a new style.

## 1. Delete on sight (these add nothing)
- **Significance markers:** "Here's the thing", "Here's the kicker", "But here's why that matters", "Let that sink in", "Read that again", "The best part?", "What struck me was", "And honestly?"
- **Throat-clearing:** "I hope this email finds you well", "I wanted to reach out", "I'm reaching out because", "My name is X and I'm the Y at Z", "Just following up", "Just checking in", "Circling back"
- **Announced emphasis:** "Importantly,", "Crucially,", "It's worth noting that", "It's important to note", "Needless to say"
- **Summary closers:** "In conclusion", "Ultimately,", "At the end of the day", "The key takeaway is", "The bottom line is"
- **Generic engagement bait:** "Thoughts?", "Agree?", "Sound familiar?", "Drop a comment below"
- **Sales-email tells:**
  - Meta-explaining your own reasoning: "so I can plan around the right thing".
  - Over-permissioning: "change anything, or ignore it if it's not useful", "no pressure".
  - Stock warm-ups: "Totally understand", "Either way", "I'd love to".
  - Scripted acknowledgements: "That's a fair concern".
  - "I want to be respectful of your time".

  Cut them all. A peer just says the thing.

## 2. Replace with plain words
| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| utilize, leverage (verb) | use |
| facilitate | help, run |
| delve into, deep dive | look at, examine |
| harness | use |
| unlock, unleash | (say what actually becomes possible) |
| empower | let, help |
| streamline | (name the step that goes away) |
| robust, seamless, cutting-edge, best-in-class, world-class, state-of-the-art | (name the specific property, or cut) |
| transformative, revolutionary, game-changing, paradigm shift | (state the actual change and its size) |
| landscape, realm, tapestry, ecosystem (as filler) | market, area, (or cut) |
| synergy, bandwidth, low-hanging fruit, move the needle, touch base | (plain description) |
| myriad, plethora, numerous, various | (give the number or name the items) |
| navigate the complexities of | handle, deal with |
| in today's fast-paced world / rapidly evolving landscape | (cut) |

## 3. Structural patterns
- **The "not X, but Y" reframe** used again and again ("It's not about tools, it's about outcomes").
- **Reflexive triplets:** every list has exactly three items, and every sentence has three adjectives.
- **Staccato fragments with no facts in them:** "Faster. Smarter. Better." Fragments that carry a number are fine and often the best line ("10 hours of battery. Under a kilo.").
- **A rhetorical question answered at once:** "The result? A 40% lift."
- **Uniform rhythm:** every paragraph the same length, sentences of similar length. Vary them: a short sentence after a long one.
- **Three or more sentences in a row starting the same way.**
- **Connective openers in every paragraph:** Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally.
- **Participle openers:** "Leveraging our platform, teams can…"
- **Over-hedging:** "may potentially help to somewhat improve". Commit to what's true, or cut what isn't.
- **Moralizing endings:** a paragraph that ends by restating the lesson.
- **Heavy em-dash use.** Many brands ban em-dashes, so follow the voice guide. Otherwise keep them rare.
- **Emoji, bold text and bullets** in a 1:1 executive email. Plain text reads as human.

## 4. Specificity test (the one that matters)
For each paragraph, ask: **could this sentence appear unchanged in a competitor's email, or in an email to a different prospect?** If yes, it is filler. Make it specific with one of these:
- a fact about *this* account or person, with a source
- a named, approved customer example
- a concrete number from the proof library
- the prospect's own words from discovery

If none is available, shorten the message. A short honest email beats a long generic one.

## 5. Executive voice specifics
- Executives write short sentences, take clear positions, and use first person when it's their experience.
- Use confidence zones for ghostwriting:
  - **Full authority:** "Here's what works."
  - **Earned perspective:** "In my experience…"
  - **Active exploration:** "We're testing…"

  Don't push an executive into stating with full authority a view they only hold tentatively.
- Never invent anecdotes, numbers or "lessons learned" for an executive's byline. Ask for the real story or leave a clearly marked gap.
